**Logic:** the science and art of reasoning well

**Reasoning:** drawing proper conclusions from other information

**Formal logic:** deals directly with reasoning, by considering the means of distinguishing between proper and improper modes of reasoning.

**Informal logic:** deals with operations of thinking that are indirectly related to reasoning, such as defining terms, relating terms to each other,

and determining relationships between statements. Informal fallacies are not formal methods of reasoning; they are also included under the branch of informal logic.

**Branches of formal logic:**

Induction: deals with arguments of likelihood and probability.

Deduction: deals with arguments that are valid or invalid.

**Main branches of deductive reasoning:**

Categorial logic: deals with **syllogisms** — a type of deductive argument in which the conclusion connects one category/term with another.

Propositional logic: connects entire propositions together in arguments.

**Laws of thought:**

The Law of Excluded Middle: any statement is either true or false

The Law of Identity: if a statement is true, then it is true

The Law of Noncontradiction: a statement cannot be both true and false

**Term:** a concept with a precise meaning expressed by one or more words

**Definition:** a statement that gives the meaning of a term

**Purposes of defining**:

(0) Showing relationships

(1) Removing ambiguity

(2) Reducing vagueness

(3) Increase vocabulary

(4) Explaining concepts theoretically

(5) Influencing attitudes

**Types of definitions:**

(1) Lexical definition: A definition that shows relationships or reduces ambiguity by providing a single, established meaning of a term

(2) Precising definition: A definition that seeks to make more precise what was previously vague, applying only to the situation in which it is used

(3) Stipulative definition: A definition that gives a specific meaning to a newly invented word, or assigns a new meaning to a word applied in a unique, undefined way; increasing the vocabulary of the language to which it is added

(4) Theoretical definition: A definition that proposes a theory about the term

(5) Persuasive definition: A definition that aims at persuading the listener a certain way toward the term

**Genus:** A term more general, broad, or abstract than the species under it

**Species:** A term that is a type, kind, or example of its genus

*Genus and species are relative terms*

**Extension:** the sum of all the individual objects to which a term applies

**Intension:** the sum of all the common attributes denoted by a termssss

**Methods of defining terms**

1. Defining by synonym
2. Defining by example
3. Defining by genus and difference: stating the genus of the term along with words distinguishing the term from every other species under the genus

**Rules for defining by genus and difference**

1. A definition should state the essential attributes of a term
2. A definition should not be circular
3. A definition should not be too broad nor too narrow
4. A definition should not be unclear or figurative
5. A definition should be s­tated positively when possible
6. A definition should be of the same part of speech as the term

**Logical argument:** a set of statements, one of which appears to be implied or supported by the others

* Premise: statements that support or imply the conclusion. *One or more per argument*
* Conclusion: the point or terminus of the argument, implied or supported by the other statements of the argument. *Only one per argument*

**Syllogism**: a deductive argument with two premises and threeterms

1. *Has three categorical statements of* A, E, I, *or* O.
2. *Contains two premises and ends with a conclusion.*
3. *Has three and only three terms.*

Major term (*P*): the predicate term of the conclusion, used in one premise

Minor term(*S*): the subject term of the conclusion, used in the *other* premise

Middle term(*M*): the term found once in each premise but not the conclusion

Major premise: the premise encapsulating the major term

Minor premise: the premise encapsulating the minor term

*There is no middle premise*.

*Standard abbreviations:*

*Therefore:* ∴

**Categorial (standard) syllogism**: a syllogism consisting of three categorical statements arranged in standard/proper order: *major premise, minor premise, conclusion*.

**Mood:** a three-letter-description of the types of categorial statements a syllogism contains when

arranged in standard order.
**Figure:** a number from 1-4 identifying the placements of the middle term in the syllogism

| Minor Premise | S is **M** | S is **M** | **M** is S | **M** is S |

**Schema:** the standard order arrangement of a syllogism, with standard abbreviations of the terms

**Form:** the mood and figure of the syllogism

**Validity:** whether or not a syllogism's premises imply the conclusion

/ the truth of the conclusion follows from the truth of the premises

/ proper reasoning is used

*Validity depends solely on the form of the argument, not on the meaning or truth of the individual statements. Certain forms are always valid; other forms are always invalid.*

A **sound** syllogism is valid and its premises are true; an **unsound** syllogism's premises aren't.

**Counterexample (to a syllogism):** A syllogism of the same form of the original, but with obviously true premises and an obviously false conclusion, in order to show the original to be invalid

**Distributed term**: within a statement, a term that refers to all members of its category.

Universal statements distribute the subject; negative statements distribute the predicate.

*Distributed Terms:*

All ***S*** is P

No ***S*** is ***P***
Some S is P

Some S is not ***P***

**The 5 rules of syllogism validity:**

1. In at least one premise, the middle term must be distributed.*Fallacy of the Undistributed Middle*
2. If a term is distributed in the conclusion, it must also be distributed in its premise.*Fallacy of Illicit Major* || *Illicit Minor*
3. A valid syllogism cannot have two negative premises.*Fallacy of Two Negative Premises*
4. A valid syllogism cannot have a negative premise and an affirmative conclusion.*Fallacy of a Negative Premise and an Affirmative Conclusion*
5. A valid syllogism cannot have two affirmative premises and a negative conclusion.*Fallacy of Two Affirmative Premises and a Negative Conclusion*

**Immediate inference:** a statement that can be inferred directly from another statement

Obverse**:** a version of a statement of the opposite quality with a negated predicate; valid for all statements.

Contrapositive**:** a version of a statement that switches and negates both the subject and the predicate. Valid for A and O statements.

***Differences between similar fallacies***

* **Apriorism vs. Composition**: inference from a member of a category to the entire category vs. inference from all parts of a whole to a whole
* **Composition vs. Division:** transferring attributes from parts to a whole vs. trasnfering attributes from a whole to its part
* **Either/or vs. Complex Question:** restricted but different options are provided vs. all options have the same outcome
* **Ad hominem vs. Bulverism:** attacking a person's character vs. attacking a person's position by how they came to it
* **Equivocation vs. Amphiboly:** usage of different definitions of a term causes vagueness vs. the grammar of the sentence causes vagueness
